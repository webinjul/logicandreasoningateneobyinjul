1. Abstract Shape Sequences & Matrices

Abstract reasoning tests whether you can recognize visual rules and predict what comes next. Do not guess based on appearance alone. Track one feature at a time.

RotationCheck whether a shape turns by a fixed angle, such as 90°, 180°, or 45°.
ReflectionLook for a horizontal, vertical, or diagonal mirror flip.
ShadingObserve whether filled and unfilled sections alternate or invert.
Addition & SubtractionCount the lines, dots, corners, or shapes being added or removed.
PositionTrack movement around corners, sides, or positions in a grid.
3×3 MatricesCompare each row and column to find a consistent transformation.

How to solve a 3×3 matrix

  • Compare the first and second figures in a row.
  • Determine what changes: rotation, shading, count, or position.
  • Apply the same rule to the third figure.
  • Check the columns too. A valid rule should be consistent.
Worked example • Rotation
→ → →

Question: What transformation occurs between each figure?

Answer: A 90° clockwise rotation. The shape stays the same; only its orientation changes.
Elimination shortcut: If the number of sides stays constant, eliminate choices with a different shape. Then compare orientation and shading.

Visual Pattern Checklist

When a figure sequence looks confusing, inspect it in this order:

  • Count the objects or parts.
  • Check whether any part rotates or flips.
  • Compare filled and unfilled regions.
  • Check whether objects move clockwise or counterclockwise.
  • Look for a rule that repeats every two or three figures.

2. Spatial & Visual Reasoning

Spatial reasoning measures how well you imagine objects changing position or form. You may need to fold paper mentally, rotate a cube, or identify a reflection.

Cube NetsA cube has six square faces. Opposite faces cannot share an edge after folding.
Paper FoldingEvery fold creates symmetry. A hole punched through folded layers appears in mirrored positions when unfolded.
Mirror ReflectionA mirror reverses left and right. The object stays the same distance from the mirror.
Water ReflectionA water reflection reverses top and bottom, as if flipped across a horizontal line.
Odd One OutCompare sides, angles, orientation, shading, and number of parts.
Figure GroupingFind the shared feature among a group, such as equal sides or matching orientation.

Cube net reminder

A common cube net has four squares in a row, with one square attached above and one below one of the middle squares.

Worked example • Paper folding

Question: A square paper is folded in half vertically. A hole is punched away from the fold. What happens when the paper is opened?

Answer: Two holes appear, mirrored across the fold line. Each folded layer receives a hole at the matching position.
Elimination shortcut: For cube nets, identify opposite faces first. A face cannot be adjacent to its opposite face after folding.

Quick Spatial Checks

  • For mirror images, compare left and right details.
  • For water reflections, compare top and bottom details.
  • For cube questions, mentally fold the net around a center square.
  • For odd-one-out questions, test one property at a time.

3. Number & Alphanumeric Series

A series is a sequence that follows a mathematical or alphabetical rule. Your goal is to discover the operation and apply it consistently.

ArithmeticAdd or subtract the same number each time. Example: 4, 7, 10, 13.
GeometricMultiply or divide by the same number. Example: 3, 6, 12, 24.
AlternatingTwo separate patterns may be interleaved. Check odd and even positions separately.
Prime NumbersNumbers with exactly two positive factors: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13.
Squares & CubesSquares: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25. Cubes: 1, 8, 27, 64.
Letter PositionsUse A=1, B=2, C=3 ... Z=26 to find alphabetical shifts.

Useful series strategies

  • Calculate the differences between consecutive terms.
  • If differences are not constant, calculate the second differences.
  • Check multiplication or division if values grow quickly.
  • Separate odd-position and even-position terms for alternating patterns.
  • For letters, convert them to alphabet positions before comparing.
Worked example • Alternating series
2, 10, 4, 20, 6, 30, ?

Step 1: Separate the odd positions: 2, 4, 6, ?

Step 2: The odd-position terms increase by 2.

Step 3: The even-position terms are 10, 20, 30.

Answer: 8. The next odd-position term is 8.
Worked example • Letter shifts
B, E, H, K, ?

B=2, E=5, H=8, K=11. Each letter advances by 3 positions.

Answer: N (position 14).
Elimination shortcut: Before trying complicated operations, check simple differences, multiplication, squares, and alternating patterns.

4. Deductive Logic & Situational Aptitude

Deductive reasoning asks you to reach a conclusion using only the facts given. Do not add assumptions that are not stated in the question.

SyllogismsUse general statements to determine what must be true. Separate facts from assumptions.
Truth-tellers & LiarsTruth-tellers always tell the truth; liars always make false statements. Test each possibility.
Ordering & RankingTranslate clues into a sequence. For example, if A is before B and B before C, then A is before C.
Seating ArrangementsDraw spaces for people. Mark left, right, next to, and opposite carefully.
If-Then StatementsIf P then Q means P guarantees Q. It does not mean Q automatically guarantees P.
Situational AptitudeChoose the response that is reasonable, responsible, fair, and supported by the situation.

Important conditional logic

  • If P → Q, then P is sufficient for Q.
  • If P → Q, then not Q means not P.
  • Do not reverse the statement: Q does not necessarily mean P.
  • Do not confuse “some” with “all.”
Worked example • Syllogism

All athletes exercise regularly. Mia is an athlete.

Question: What must be true?

Answer: Mia exercises regularly. This follows directly from the two statements.
Worked example • Ordering

Ben is taller than Ana. Ana is taller than Carlo.

Question: Who is the tallest?

Answer: Ben. The order is Ben → Ana → Carlo, from tallest to shortest.
Elimination shortcut: Remove answers that contradict a given fact, introduce an unstated assumption, or claim more than the clues prove.
